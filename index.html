Notes & method

Turnover

A space is not free the moment a patient walks out of it, and the lane is charged for turning it over. That charge falls hardest on the layouts that move patients through fastest, because they turn their spaces over most often, and on room-heavy footprints, because a room takes ten times a chair. The chair figure applies wherever a chair is used: both sides of a divided lane, and the whole of the pooled one. The rooms-first layout has rooms by definition, and a divided lane can say its assessment side is rooms — the control under Spaces — which is what 8 rooms + 10 chairs does.

Where the complaint groups come from

Chief complaints as triage records them, grouped by our shared normaliser so Febrile and Fever are one complaint rather than two. Volumes and test rates are measured per complaint; the SHAPE of the service times is department-wide, scaled to each complaint's own mean. The mix itself is measured on 15:00–23:00 and applied to whatever window you pick. It is close for any daytime window, and exact for the 15:00–23:00 one it was measured on — but an overnight lane would see a different mix from the one this assumes.

The exclusion list — what the complaint field can and cannot see

Presentations that need a door for the initial evaluation, and so wait for a room rather than take a chair. Tap room on a complaint's row to add or remove it — tapping the complaint itself takes it out of the lane instead. The three ticked to start are the ones Blake named that a chief complaint can actually carry — abdominal pain, rash, and the genital complaints as a group. That is a starting point, not an answer: this is the list the MD group has to establish and sign off, and it is a clinical judgement rather than anything the data can settle.

How the must-have-a-room share is built up

Three measured parts, then one you set: the complaints you tick, the patients needing an interpreter, and the patients arriving with a sibling — then this share of whoever is left. The last one is a rate on the remainder, so the four figures do not sum to the total. The total above the list is what the page runs, and it is the number worth arguing about.

What this does and does not know

  • Where the numbers come from. Arrival pattern, how long people occupy a space, and the half who need a test are all measured from Mary Bridge ESI 4/5 patients, 2025 onward. Nothing here is invented except the assumptions flagged in the next item.
  • Families arrive together, and the lane takes the whole family or none of it. 5.61% of the children arriving between 15:00 and 22:00 — the window this was measured on, whatever hours you give the lane — arrive with a sibling — mostly pairs, a few threes. That is measured, not assumed: reshuffling arrival times among the same surnames puts the coincidence rate near zero. It matters because two siblings need two spaces at the same moment, and a lane that seats one and leaves the other outside is not something the department does. Modelling them as a party rather than as two unrelated arrivals costs about a minute per patient at six spaces, and less as the lane gets bigger — the cost lands where the lane is tight, which is exactly where these layouts differ. It is a floor: a family whose children have different surnames is invisible to the measurement.
  • Two assumptions, and both are controls rather than constants. The first is how long a patient keeps the assessment space before moving on — the slider under Timing. Nothing measures it: what we can measure is that a test patient's first order lands 42 minutes after rooming, when it follows rooming at all — one in five arrives in the space with it already placed. The 67-minute reference this page also quotes is that 42 plus an assumed 25 minutes after the order — nothing timestamps when a patient could actually move, so that 25 was chosen, not measured. Neither number is a decision anyone made, and neither exists at all for the half who need no test. It is the number that decides the best split, so move it and watch the split move. The second is what happens, in the combined-zone layouts, to the half of patients who need no test at all. Nothing measures that either, and it decides whether two assessment chairs can work. Try both ways.
  • The lane closes when you close it, and that is modelled. Anyone still waiting for a space goes to the main department. Without that rule the page quietly served its backlog in overtime — a six-space lane ran until 09:04 the next morning and spent 45% of its work outside its own hours, which made every layout's worst hour land at 22:00 and read like a demand peak. It was the overtime bucket.
  • Read the wait and the patients sent away together. The wait already includes the patients sent to the main department — but only for what they waited in the lane, up to closing. What they wait after that is not in it; the score is what charges that. So a layout that sends people away looks worse on the wait, not better: at its most crowded those patients are the ones waiting longest. The wait is the lane's own queue; the score is the whole day.
  • The score prices everyone who arrives, including the ones you turn down. Every other number here improves if the lane takes fewer patients — narrow the criteria, or let the queue spill past closing, and the average among those you served looks better while the day is unchanged. So the score charges the patients a lane does not serve what they actually get instead: today's wait for a room in the main department, by hour — 32 minutes at 15:00 rising to 107 at 22:00. Accept nobody and you score exactly the status quo. There is no way to win by shrinking.
  • There is no “no fast track” comparison, and there cannot be. The department has run one throughout the period measured here, so no lane-free day exists to compare against. This page briefly offered one, built by excluding the patients who went through today's pod — it was withdrawn because 53% of the walkouts in those hours have no care area recorded at all, so they fell into the “no fast track” group by bookkeeping rather than by outcome, and what remained was the pod's overflow rather than a counterfactual.
  • The bar is today, and today already has a fast track. An ESI 4/5 patient arriving between 15:00 and 23:00 waits 58.1 minutes on average now. The score compares against 50.0 rather than 58.1, because it prices every ESI 4/5 patient of the day and the quieter daytime hours are in that average too. Both are today; they differ only in which patients they count. But Seal Rock — the area running as the fast track since July 2026 — is already taking 67–85% of them until 19:00, and the department's whole hourly escalation — 32 minutes at 15:00 to 107 at 22:00, measured across the full window rather than this pod's own six weeks — runs the same way. Across its open hours the middle patient waits the same either way — a median of 22 minutes for its patients and 22 for everyone else — and the gap opens at 20:00, once it stops taking new patients: the median for everyone else goes to 86 minutes while the few still placed there wait 19. That boundary is a decision, not a property of the building — the lane winds down from 19:00 so that everyone is out by close. Whether a proposed lane is added to today's pod or replaces it therefore changes what it should be compared against, and this page does not try to settle that — for the reason in the bullet above, there is no lane-free bar to offer.
  • The clock starts at the door. A patient cannot be put anywhere before registration and the start of triage — 8 minutes on average here, barely varying across the day. That is added to every lane wait, because the figure it is compared against — today's arrival-to-room — contains it too. Without it the lane was being credited with 8 minutes it never saves.
  • It assumes the main department is unaffected. Taking work out of it should make its wait shorter, which this does not model — so if anything the score understates what excluding patients costs.
  • Whether narrowing helps depends on the layout, not on a rule. At ten spaces a lane taking everyone beats one taking only the easy complaints. At six it is the other way round, because six cannot absorb them. Do not carry either result across to a different footprint.
  • The split follows the assessment time — it is not a fixed answer. The best split at ten spaces runs 2+8 if the assessment ends at 20 minutes and 6+4 if it ends at 90, tracking how the visit divides between the two sides. What barely moves is the score you can achieve: about three minutes across that whole range, provided the split matches the time. So the question to settle is when an assessment is finished, not which split is right in the abstract.
  • There is no pass mark, on purpose. Nothing measured here says how long a patient may reasonably wait, so the page does not pretend to know. A line drawn at a number nobody can justify turns every comparison into a verdict against it. Compare the layouts with each other, and decide what is acceptable yourselves.
  • Waits are averages, and small differences are noise. Most patients wait nothing at all, and half a day's delay can land in a single hour — which is why the worst hour is shown separately. Two layouts a minute or two apart are not reliably in that order; rerun them and they can swap.
  • The rooms-first layout is scored on delay, and delay is not the argument for it. "Rooms first, chairs only when rooms are full" was proposed for privacy, for a more thorough initial evaluation behind a door, and for cutting the handoffs and forgotten tasks that come from moving a patient mid-visit. None of those are in this model. What the page can tell you is what the rule costs in minutes — and the answer is that it costs very little while there are enough rooms, and a great deal when there are not. Read the score here as "what am I giving up to do this", not as a verdict on whether to do it.
  • "Must have a room" is a floor, not a measurement. The share is set from the chief complaints the record can actually see. The rest of the reasons a patient needs a door — a sensitive history, a family that is frightened or angry — are triage judgements that appear nowhere in the data. So the true share is higher than the default by an amount only the people doing triage can put a number on. Move the slider to yours.
  • A room and a chair are treated as taking the same time. If an evaluation behind a door is genuinely faster or more thorough than one in a chair, that is not modelled — nothing measures it. It would help the rooms-first layout if true. The same gap applies to an interpreted encounter, which plausibly runs longer than the measured mean: the model sends those patients to a room but still gives them an average visit, so it captures the space they need and not the time.
  • It cannot see the ward. Who is watching a patient waiting on a result, whether an open chair is right for a given child, and what happens at 23:00 are all real and none of them are in here. If pooling wins on this screen and loses on the floor, the floor is right.

Genital complaints are their own row now — urinary ones are not in it

The sensitive-area half of Blake’s rule has its own row — 18 complaint labels merged into one row, 0.87% of the lane — small enough to mark as needing a room without putting a quarter of the department in one.

Urinary complaints are deliberately not in that group. Dysuria, hematuria and urinary frequency or retention — 205 visits, 1.3% — do not usually need a sensitive exam, which is the whole reason the group exists (physicians). Dysuria is not on the list for the same reason: it was Blake’s sensitive-exam entry, and that is the rationale the physicians withdrew. It is still there to tick if the group wants it back on other grounds.

An interpreter is measured, not guessed

11.4% of this lane records a preferred language other than English — taken at registration, not judged at the bedside, and recorded for 99.7% of the visits in the window across 18 languages. Reading that as needing an interpreter is a judgement, not a count — but it is a recorded field rather than a guess, which is what separates it from the share below. An interpreted evaluation has to be audible and private, and an interpreter is often placed physically at the bedside; a chair in an open bay is neither.

It is ticked above as its own criterion, and it is not a flat percentage. Each complaint carries its own measured rate — from 4.2% to 24.1% — so narrowing the criteria moves it, as it should. It applies only to whoever the ticked complaints leave behind, so a patient is never counted twice.

A sibling in the room, not a chair each

5.61% of the children arriving between 15:00 and 22:00 come with a sibling — that window, not whatever hours you give the lane. It is measured in the record rather than assumed — reshuffling arrival times among the same surnames puts the coincidence rate near zero. They are ticked as needing a room, because a family goes into one room together rather than into separate chairs across the lane, and because the lane takes the whole family or none of it.

It is a floor: a family whose children have different surnames cannot be seen in the data at all, so the true share is higher by an amount nothing here can measure.

And the part no chief complaint can capture

Two reasons a patient needs a door remain uncodable, and the slider below is now for those alone:

  • A sensitive history — taken in the open, it is not taken.
  • A family who needs a door — frightened, angry or simply at the end of their patience. A triage judgement made in the moment and written down nowhere.

Neither will ever be a field, so this one stays a number only the group can set. It starts at zero, which is certainly too low — but it is a smaller job than it looks, because the two shares that could be measured already have been.

Two of the reasons Blake names are deliberately absent from all of this: child-abuse evaluations and mental-health presentations. Neither is missing from the data — they are missing from this population. Every encounter behind this page is ESI 4/5, and neither is triaged there. A child-abuse concern is usually coded as Well Child, so it looks present and is not; recognised at triage, it is triaged up. Mental-health presentations do not reach ESI 4/5 at all. Those patients never arrive in this lane, so counting either here would pad the share with people the lane never sees — and the Well Child arrivals in the list above are ordinary low-acuity visits, not this.

Both still need a door. They are someone else's flow problem, not this lane's. And a concern that surfaces only after rooming is a third case again — no triage-time routing rule can act on that one either way.
